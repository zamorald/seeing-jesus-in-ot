Patterns of Christ in the Story of Joseph

Sent By The Father

Genesis 37:2, 13–14

…Joseph, being seventeen years old, was pasturing the flock with his brothers. He was a boy with the sons of…his father’s wives. And Joseph brought a bad report of them to their father….
And Israel said to Joseph, Are not your brothers pasturing the flock at Shechem? Come, I will send you to them. And he said to him, Here I am. So he said to him, Go now, see if it is well with your brothers and with the flock, and bring me word.

Jacob not only counts his son Joseph a trustworthy messenger, but he entrusts Joseph with assessing the status both of his brothers and of his father’s flocks.

As a trustworthy messenger, Joseph is duty-bound to give his father the truth about his brothers’ behavior in the field and the welfare of the animals in their care.

In the same way, Jesus, duty-bound to the truth, was sent by the Father to see how His brothers (the leaders of Israel) and His Father’s flock (the people) were doing:

John 5:22–23, 30

“The Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son, that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father. Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him….
I can do nothing on my own. As I hear, I judge, and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me.”

Just as Joseph was ready and eager to accomplish whatever his father asked of Him, Jesus also sought not His own will, but the will of His Father who sent Him.

John 8:42–47

Jesus said to them, If God were your Father, you would love me, for I came from God and I am here. I came not of my own accord, but he sent me. Why do you not understand what I say? It is because you cannot bear to hear my word. You are of your father the devil, and your will is to do your father’s desires…. But because I tell the truth, you do not believe me. Which one of you convicts me of sin? If I tell the truth, why do you not believe me? Whoever is of God hears the words of God. The reason why you do not hear them is that you are not of God.

No matter how truthful it was, Joseph’s report—as Jesus’s—likely displeased the others.

His Father’s Favoritism Made His Brothers Jealous

Genesis 37:3–4

Now Israel loved Joseph more than any other of his sons, because he was the son of his old age. And he made him a robe of many colors. But when his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, they hated him and could not speak peacefully to him.

The pattern here: God’s chosen servant often garners the ire of the existing authorities when he comes with authority from heaven to do God’s will.

This pattern is clearly at play when Daniel comes to power in Babylon:

Daniel 6:3–4

Then this Daniel became distinguished above all the other high officials and satraps, because an excellent spirit was in him. And the king planned to set him over the whole kingdom. Then the high officials and the satraps sought to find a ground for complaint against Daniel….

Though Daniel in fact is a faithful servant both of God and the king of Babylon, the high officials did not want to see him promoted to second in command—above themselves—or his Jewish brothers put into positions of power. (Joseph, of course, will be promoted to second in command by Pharaoh, and ultimately the Father puts all things beneath Jesus’s feet.)

In the gospels, the shrewd Roman governor Pilate was able to discern that the leaders in Jerusalem had betrayed Jesus because they were envious of Him:

Mark 15:10

[Pilate] perceived it was out of envy that the chief priests had delivered [Jesus] up.

Revelation: Joseph Will Reign

Genesis 37:5–11

Now Joseph had a dream, and when he told it to his brothers they hated him even more. He said to them, Hear this dream that I have dreamed: Behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and behold, my sheaf arose and stood upright. And behold, your sheaves gathered around it and bowed down to my sheaf. His brothers said to him, Are you indeed to reign over us? Or are you indeed to rule over us? So they hated him even more for his dreams and for his words. Then he dreamed another dream and told it to his brothers and said, Behold, I have dreamed another dream. Behold, the sun, the moon, and eleven stars were bowing down to me. But when he told it to his father and to his brothers, his father rebuked him and said to him, What is this dream that you have dreamed? Shall I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow ourselves to the ground before you? And his brothers were jealous of him, but his father kept the saying in mind.

Are you indeed to rule over us?

There is a pattern of rejection throughout Scripture when someone proves more just than his peers, his teachers, or generally anyone whose place in society is thought to be higher than his.

Genesis 19:9

…And they said, This fellow came to sojourn, and he has become the judge…!

The reading from Genesis above provides an example of that pattern. While residing within Sodom, Lot tries to prevent the men of the town from assaulting two angels staying with him. Far from pacifying the crowd, Lot’s attempts at a peaceful resolution provoke a rebuke from his hosts.

The same pattern can be seen in Moses’s rejection at the hands of his fellow Israelites. The text records an almost verbatim quote of what Joseph’s brothers had said to him: “Who made you a prince and judge over us?”

Exodus 2:11–14

One day, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his people and looked on their burdens, and he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his people. He looked this way and that, and seeing no one, he struck down the Egyptian and hid him in the sand. When he went out the next day, behold, two Hebrews were struggling together. And he said to the man in the wrong, Why do you strike your companion? He answered, Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you mean to kill me as you killed the Egyptian…?

Stephen, a deacon of the church in Jerusalem and a man full of the Spirit, referred to this same pattern. He was drawing a comparison to the rejection of Jesus in first-century Jerusalem:

Acts 7:23–27, 35

When [Moses] was forty years old, it came into his heart to visit his brothers, the children of Israel. And seeing one of them being wronged, he defended the oppressed man and avenged him by striking down the Egyptian. He supposed that his brothers would understand that God was giving them salvation by his hand, but they did not understand. And on the following day he appeared to them as they were quarreling and tried to reconcile them, saying, ‘Men, you are brothers. Why do you wrong each other?’ But the man who was wronging his neighbor thrust him aside, saying, ‘Who made you a ruler and a judge over us…?’
This Moses, whom they rejected, saying, ‘Who made you a ruler and a judge?’—this man God sent as both ruler and redeemer by the hand of the angel who appeared to him in the bush.

Two of Stephen’s comments demand our notice.

First, he says Moses thought his fellow Israelites would understand God was giving them salvation through him. It would have been reasonable for the Israelites to draw the conclusion God was planning to grant them deliverance from Egypt at that moment; after all, a Hebrew child who had been marked for extermination had instead become a member of Pharaoh’s household, reached adulthood, and was now advocating on behalf his countrymen. (A similar thing would come about in the cases of Esther, Daniel, and soon an Ethiopian eunuch…!)

By saying Moses thought they would understand, Stephen is telling us Moses was disappointed. The meaning is clear: Jesus looked for a better reception among the children of Israel, but they did not understand!

Stephen’s second comment we note emphatically answers the question posed by those who rejected Moses. Who made Moses the ruler? Stephen’s answer: God did. Those listening to Stephen knew it was true Israel had initially rejected Moses despite God having appeared to him in the burning bush, and that Moses was nonetheless the one who would eventually lead them out of Egypt.

The meaning here is also abundantly clear: the rulers in Jerusalem had rejected Jesus initially, but God really did send Him, and He would still be the “Ruler and Redeemer” despite their initial rejection. As for Me, I have set My King on Zion… (Psalm 2:6).

Shall I and your mother…indeed come to bow?

In a typical household in ancient Israel, the father would be head, and the children subject to their mother, with the oldest son being the most responsible among his siblings. But the dream of Joseph reveals a disruption of the rule of family: Joseph—not a parent and not even the first-born—will be ruler of all.

So, too, Jesus will become greater than His own parents, who also did not at first accept—or at least, fully understand—His coming power. And while He is their firstborn, His Law comes second in time, centuries after Moses.

Luke 2:33–35

And his father and his mother marveled at what was said about him. And Simeon blessed them and said to Mary his mother, Behold, this child is appointed for the fall and rising of many in Israel, and for a sign that is opposed (and a sword will pierce through your own soul also), so that thoughts from many hearts may be revealed.

Before the conversation above took place—even before Jesus was born—Joseph and Mary had both received a visit from an angel of God (Matthew 1:18–21; Luke 1:26–27, 32–33), telling them amazing things about the Child’s role in Israel. The angel who speaks to Joseph uses language that reminds us of the Judges of ancient Israel’s past; Mary learns Jesus will be the Son of the Most High and reign on David’s throne forever.

Despite the angels setting very high expectations, Joseph and Mary still marveled at what the prophet Simeon said. And Simeon even tells Mary her own soul will continue to have hard choices to make in the future.

Even a little later, 12-year-old Jesus has already begun to excel His parents and cling to His heavenly Father, as recorded in Luke:

Luke 2:43–50

And when the feast was ended, as they were returning, the boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem. His parents did not know it, but supposing him to be in the group they went a day’s journey, but then they began to search for him among their relatives and acquaintances, and when they did not find him, they returned to Jerusalem, searching for him. After three days they found him in the temple, sitting among the teachers, listening to them and asking them questions. And all who heard him were amazed at his understanding and his answers. And when his parents saw him, they were astonished. And his mother said to him, Son, why have you treated us so? Behold, your father and I have been searching for you in great distress. And he said to them, Why were you looking for me? Did you not know that I must be in my Father’s house? And they did not understand the saying that he spoke to them.

Again, just as Jacob had thought Joseph ought to stand down and remain subject to him and Rachel, so also Jesus’s parents did not understand He was overtaking them in holiness.

And although we know two of the Lord’s brothers (James and Jude) eventually came to believe, His brothers as a whole certainly were not accepting of Him during His time on earth:

John 7:5

Not even his brothers believed in him.

Luke 8:19–21

Then his mother and his brothers came to him, but they could not reach him because of the crowd. And he was told, Your mother and your brothers are standing outside, desiring to see you. But he answered them, My mother and my brothers are those who hear the word of God and do it.

The teaching of Jesus draws a sharp distinction between earthly and spiritual family—and prioritizes the spiritual. The crowd knows His family wishes to “pull rank” and summon Him away from His teaching duties, so He makes it clear His mission and those who join Him in it are more important even than immediate family. “…There is a friend who sticks closer than a brother” (Proverbs 18:24).

Matthew 10:34–37

“Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth. I have not come to bring peace, but a sword. For I have come to set a man against his father, and a daughter against her mother, and a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. And a person’s enemies will be those of his own household. Whoever loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me, and whoever loves son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me.”

Peace did not characterize the days of Jacob and his sons until they were reconciled to the power and kingdom of Joseph.

We, too, must be reconciled to Jesus and enter His kingdom to know the peace of God. And if we cannot be reconciled to Him, we cannot be counted worthy of being in His presence, either—not even His own household was accorded that favor.

But his father kept the saying in mind…

Even though he did not fully understand the strange things he was hearing from his son Joseph, Jacob is said to have held on to Joseph’s words. Jacob is, after all, a man of faith, and he has seen God do many things people did not think possible.

So, too, Jesus’s mother kept a remembrance of the words and events of His infancy and childhood:

Luke 2:18–19

And all who heard it wondered at what the shepherds told them. But Mary treasured up all these things, pondering them in her heart.

Luke 2:50–51

And they did not understand the saying that he spoke to them. And he went down with them and came to Nazareth and was submissive to them. And his mother treasured up all these things in her heart.

Mary herself may well have been the witness interviewed by Luke and the other gospel writers to garner the details of His birth.

They Conspired to Kill Him

Genesis 37:18–20

They saw [Joseph] from afar, and before he came near to them they conspired against him to kill him. They said to one another, Here comes this dreamer. Come now, let us kill him and throw him into one of the pits. Then we will say that a fierce animal has devoured him, and we will see what will become of his dreams.

In this pattern, there is a conspiracy to remove God’s servant, throw him underground, and blame someone else for his disappearance.

We will see what will become of his dreams…

Joseph’s brothers intend to kill him in order to prevent the fulfillment of his prophetic dreams that revealed he would reign.

In Daniel also we see the nation’s leaders form a conspiracy to destroy the servant of God who can interpret dreams. Their motivation is not anything Daniel has done wrong; rather, they fear displacement when the king promotes Daniel above them.

Daniel 6:3–7

Then this Daniel became distinguished above all the other high officials and satraps, because an excellent spirit was in him. And the king planned to set him over the whole kingdom. Then the high officials and the satraps sought to find a ground for complaint against Daniel with regard to the kingdom, but they could find no ground for complaint or any fault, because he was faithful, and no error or fault was found in him. Then these men said, We shall not find any ground for complaint against this Daniel unless we find it in connection with the law of his God. Then these high officials and satraps came by agreement to the king and said to him, “O King Darius, live forever! All the high officials of the kingdom, the prefects and the satraps, the counselors and the governors are agreed that the king should establish an ordinance and enforce an injunction, that whoever makes petition to any god or man for thirty days, except to you, O king, shall be cast into the den of lions.”

The rulers of Jerusalem also intend to prevent Jesus from having His rightful place above them as the Son of the Master:

Matthew 21:33, 37–39, 45

[Jesus said,] Hear another parable. There was a master of a house who planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower and leased it to tenants, and went into another country….
Finally he sent his son [to the tenants to get his fruit], saying, ‘They will respect my son.’ But when the tenants saw the son, they said to themselves, ‘This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance.’ And they took him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him….
When the chief priests and the Pharisees heard his parables, they perceived that he was speaking about them.

Ironically, the conspirators against Jesus justify destroying the rightful Heir by claiming it will preserve the rest of the family:

John 11:47–50, 53

So the chief priests and the Pharisees gathered the council and said, What are we to do? For this man performs many signs. If we let him go on like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation. But one of them, Caiaphas, who was high priest that year, said to them, You know nothing at all. Nor do you understand that it is better for you that one man should die for the people, not that the whole nation should perish.
So from that day on they made plans to put him to death.

In the end, their claim proves ironic because their action to destroy the intended victim in fact does result in the preservation of the entire family—just not in the way they intended.

Jacob and his sons and their families only survived the great famine because Joseph was ruler over Egypt. And Joseph would never have become ruler of Egypt if he had not earlier been sold there as a slave by his brothers.

Genesis 50:20

“As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today.”

The irony of the High Priest’s statement is pointed out by John when he records it:

John 11:51–52

He did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad.

We will say a fierce animal has devoured him…

The conspirators intend to cover up the truth about the servant’s death.

Joseph and Daniel both conform to this pattern: he will be cast into a pit, supposed to have been killed by a wild animal.

Genesis 37:31–33

Then they took Joseph’s robe and slaughtered a goat and dipped the robe in the blood. And they sent the robe of many colors and brought it to their father and said, This we have found; please identify whether it is your son’s robe or not. And he identified it and said, It is my son’s robe. A fierce animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn to pieces.

Jerusalem’s leaders intended to place the blame for Jesus’s death on Rome (the “eagle”), and they went to considerable effort to hide the truth about His death—that He overcame it!

Matthew 27:62–66

The next day…, the chief priests and the Pharisees gathered before Pilate and said, Sir, we remember how that impostor said, while he was still alive, ‘After three days I will rise.’ Therefore order the tomb to be made secure until the third day, lest his disciples go and steal him away and tell the people, ‘He has risen from the dead,’ and the last fraud will be worse than the first. Pilate said to them, You have a guard of soldiers. Go, make it as secure as you can. So they went and made the tomb secure by sealing the stone and setting a guard.

Matthew 28:2–4, 11–15

And behold, there was a great earthquake, for an angel of the Lord descended from heaven and came and rolled back the stone and sat on it. His appearance was like lightning, and his clothing white as snow. And for fear of him the guards trembled and became like dead men.
…Some of the guard went into the city and told the chief priests all that had taken place. And when they had assembled with the elders and taken counsel, they gave a sufficient sum of money to the soldiers and said, Tell people, ‘His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep.’ And if this comes to the governor’s ears, we will satisfy him and keep you out of trouble. So they took the money and did as they were directed. And this story has been spread among the Jews to this day.

Both Joseph’s brothers and the leaders of Jerusalem knew their victim was in reality still alive when they put out their false reports of his demise.

And both Daniel and Joseph survived their ordeals; both foreshadow the resurrection of Jesus.

(Apparent) Death

Genesis 37:23–24

So when Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of his robe, the robe of many colors that he wore. And they took him and threw him into a pit. The pit was empty; there was no water in it.

Joseph’s brothers stripped him of his robe at the time of his (planned) death; Roman soldiers stripped Jesus of His garments at the time of His crucifixion.

John 19:23–24 (quoting Psalm 22:18)

When the soldiers had crucified Jesus, they took his garments and divided them into four parts, one part for each soldier; also his tunic. But the tunic was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom, so they said to one another, “Let us not tear it, but cast lots for it to see whose it shall be.” This was to fulfill the Scripture which says,
    “They divided my garments among them,
        and for my clothing they cast lots.”

Joseph’s brothers threw him into an unused pit; Jesus was buried in an unused tomb.

John 19:41–42

Now in the place where he was crucified there was a garden, and in the garden a new tomb in which no one had yet been laid…. They laid Jesus there.

Betrayal

Genesis 37:26–28

Then Judah said to his brothers, What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood? Come, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let not our hand be upon him, for he is our brother, our own flesh. And his brothers listened to him. Then Midianite traders passed by. And they drew Joseph up and lifted him out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty shekels of silver. They took Joseph to Egypt.

Judah

It was Joseph’s brother Judah who suggested that they sell him into slavery to make a profit.

It must be noted that while the names ‘Judah’ and ‘Judas’ seem to be different in translation, both in fact translate the same Hebrew name. When translating Hebrew into Greek (such as the Hebrew Old Testament into the Greek Septuagint or the Gospels), Hebrew names ending in ‘-h’ are typically transliterated with an ‘-s’ instead.

Thus, the Hebrew ‘Judah’ becomes ‘Judas’ in the Gospels.

Let us sell him…for twenty shekels of silver

And it was indeed Judas (Hebrew ‘Judah’) who would sell Jesus for a few silver pieces.

Matthew 26:14–16

Then one of the twelve, whose name was Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests and said, “What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?” And they paid him thirty pieces of silver. And from that moment he sought an opportunity to betray him.

Let not our hand be upon him…

Joseph’s brothers wanted to distance themselves from their brother’s murder by using foreigners to get rid of him. Jesus’s betrayers used the Roman government to bring about His death.

Matthew 27:1–2

When morning came, all the chief priests and the elders of the people took counsel against Jesus to put him to death. And they bound him and led him away and delivered him over to Pilate the governor.

John 19:12,15–16

…Pilate sought to release him, but the Jews cried out, “If you release this man, you are not Caesar’s friend. Everyone who makes himself a king opposes Caesar.”
Pilate said to them, “Shall I crucify your King?” The chief priests answered, “We have no king but Caesar.” So he delivered him over to them to be crucified….

Leveraging foreign power is not the only way people tried to escape blame for Christ’s death, either.

  • Judas tried to give the price on Jesus’s head back to the chief priests and elders (Matthew 27:3–4);
  • the chief priests and elders used the money to buy a field instead of taking it into the treasury (Matthew 27:6–7);
  • all the disciples fled at His arrest (Matthew 26:56), and Peter would later claim not even to know Jesus (Matthew 26:69–75);
  • Pilate tried to force the people’s hand by offering them a notorious criminal to kill instead (Matthew 27:15–18);
  • Pilate washed his hands before the crowd to claim innocence in the matter (Matthew 27:24);
  • among many other examples.
